# Theological Anthropology
Theological anthropology is the study of humanity in relation to God. It considers human origins, nature, purpose, dignity, sin, and destiny in the light of divine revelation.
## What Man Is?
### The Imago Dei (Image of God)
Human beings are created in the image of God. Bearing God's image includes:
- Resembling the character of God.
- Resembling, in a creaturely and limited way, the attributes of God.
- Exercising authority, accepting responsibility, and having dominion over creation under God's authority.
- Possessing inherent worth and dignity.
- Representing God's authority and presence in the world.
An idol placed in a pagan temple represented the presence and authority of its deity. In an analogous sense, a human being is a living, walking "image" of God: a representative commissioned to make God's rule and presence visible within creation. This analogy does not make humanity divine or an object of worship; it describes humanity's representative role.
### The Telos of Man
The *telos* of humanity is its intended purpose or end. Human beings are called:
- To glorify God and enjoy him forever.
- To know God and to be known by him.
- To build his kingdom.
- To make disciples.
#### Shared Purpose and Personal Calling
There can be too much emphasis on finding an individual "personal calling." Because all human beings are image-bearers, much of our purpose overlaps. Our common calling provides the framework within which individual vocations, gifts, relationships, and circumstances take shape.
## Reflection
- What does faithfulness to this shared purpose look like for each person in the particular places, responsibilities, and areas of life in which God has placed them?
- What are some obstacles holding us back from our purpose?
- How should the understanding that other people are image bearers impact how we treat them?
## What Has Man Become?
### The Fall
This section focuses on the trees in Eden and the nature of the first disobedience. The extent and effects of human corruption, including the doctrine of total depravity, will be considered separately at a later stage.
#### The Generosity of God's Command
God's command was given in a setting of abundance rather than deprivation. Adam and Eve were permitted to eat from the many trees of the garden; only the tree of the knowledge of good and evil was forbidden (Genesis 2:16–17). The prohibition should therefore be understood against the background of God's generosity.
#### The Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil
The meaning of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil has been understood in several ways. One important view is that "knowledge" here includes **experiential knowledge**: knowledge deeper than the mere intellectual apprehension of facts.

Scripture can use *knowing* for intimate or personal experience. For example, Adam "knew" Eve and she conceived a child (Genesis 4:1). Applied to the tree, this suggests that Adam and Eve already could have known the distinction between good and evil through their relationship with God and their trust in his good character. They did not need to experience evil personally in order to recognize it as evil.

The difference can be illustrated by a parent warning a child not to put a finger into an electrical socket. The child can know that doing so is bad by trusting the parent, or the child can disobey and acquire painful experiential knowledge. Adam and Eve chose to know evil through participation rather than trusting God's knowledge and command.

The temptation was also a desire to "be like God" (Genesis 3:5). Instead of receiving moral knowledge within a dependent relationship with God, they sought it on their own terms. Their act was therefore a rejection of trust in God's goodness and authority.
#### The Tree of Life
The tree of life stood in Eden alongside the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 2:9). After the Fall, humanity was barred from it (Genesis 3:22–24). It appears again in Revelation as part of the restored creation, where it signifies life, healing, and unhindered fellowship with God (Revelation 2:7; 22:2, 14).
## Original Sin and Guilt
Christians broadly agree that Adam's sin affected the whole human race, but they differ over how his guilt relates to his descendants. Two broad approaches are useful for this discussion.
### Imputed Guilt and Representative Headship
On this view, Adam acted as the representative or covenant head of humanity. His first sin is therefore counted, or imputed, to those whom he represented. Human beings enter the world already bearing Adam's guilt, in addition to inheriting a corrupted nature that produces their own sins.

This view commonly understands Romans 5:12–19 as drawing a parallel between two representative heads: Adam's disobedience is counted to those who are in Adam, while Christ's obedience and righteousness are counted to those who are in Christ.
### Guilt through Participation
On the participatory view, human beings share in the fallen condition introduced by Adam and become guilty as they participate in sin themselves. Adam's sin brings corruption, mortality, and a sinful environment to humanity, but guilt is connected to each person's actual participation in sin rather than being transferred as Adam's personal culpability at birth.

This view can also appeal to the Adam–Christ parallel. Just as a person comes to share in Adam's sin and its condemnation through participation in fallen humanity, a person shares in Christ's righteousness through participation in him. Union with Christ is therefore more than receiving an external legal status; it is incorporation into Christ and participation in his righteous life.
### The Central Question
The difference concerns the basis on which Adam's descendants are considered guilty: whether Adam's guilt is immediately imputed to humanity through his representative role, or whether human beings become guilty through their own participation in the sinful reality Adam inaugurated. Both views seek to account for the solidarity between Adam and humanity and for Paul's parallel between Adam and Christ.
